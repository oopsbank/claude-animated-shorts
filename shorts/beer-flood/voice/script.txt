In 1814, a street in London was hit by a tidal wave. Of beer.

The Horse Shoe Brewery made porter, a dark beer, in wooden vats taller than a house. The biggest stood twenty-two feet high, held together by iron hoops.

One afternoon, a clerk named George Crick noticed that one of those hoops had slipped. Seven hundred pounds of iron. He wrote a note. He was told it was fine.

An hour later, the vat burst.

The wave smashed the vat beside it. Then the next one. A brick wall came down. Over a million litres of beer rolled into St Giles, one of the poorest streets in London, where whole families lived in cellars.

The cellars filled first. Eight people never got out.

Here is why one hoop mattered. Liquid pushes outward, and the deeper it gets, the harder it pushes. Each hoop is like a belt after a huge dinner. Lose one notch, and the rest have to hold more. Then, pop.

The court called it an Act of God, so nobody was to blame. Luckily for the brewery, the government even refunded the tax on the lost beer. Unluckily for the government, that was about seven thousand pounds.

And London learned a lesson that still holds today. When seven hundred pounds of iron falls off something, it is not fine.

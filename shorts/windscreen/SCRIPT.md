# What If Your Plane's Windscreen Blew Out? (British Airways Flight 5390, 1990)

## Facts (with sources)
- 10 June 1990, BAC One-Eleven G-BJRT, Birmingham -> Malaga, 81 passengers + 6 crew (87 aboard).
- Left windscreen separated at ~17,300 ft while climbing. Captain Tim Lancaster was sucked out of his seat and
  pinned over the top of the fuselage; flight attendant Nigel Ogden held his legs; when Ogden tired,
  chief steward John Heward and Simon Rogers took over.
- The crew believed he was dead but did not let go, fearing the body would hit the wing, engine or tail.
- First Officer Alastair Atchison landed at Southampton, 22 minutes after the blowout.
- Lancaster survived: frostbite, fractures of the right arm, left thumb and right wrist, bruising, shock.
  He returned to flying within months.
- Cause: the windscreen had been replaced 27 hours before. 84 of 90 bolts were 0.026 in (0.66 mm) too small
  in diameter, 6 were too short. The shift manager matched the bolts to the removed ones, which were also
  wrong, instead of checking the parts catalogue; nobody independently checked the job.
- The One-Eleven windscreen is fitted from the outside, so cabin pressure pushes it outward against the bolts.
Sources: AAIB Aircraft Accident Report 1/92; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways_Flight_5390;
https://skybrary.aero/accidents-and-incidents/ba11-en-route-didcot-uk-1990; https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/326018

## Script (English, ~210 words)
What if the window in front of your pilot just... left?

June 1990. A British Airways jet climbs out of Birmingham, off to Spain. Eighty-one passengers. Six crew.

At seventeen thousand feet, the windscreen blows out. The captain, Tim Lancaster, is sucked out of his seat
and folded over the roof of the plane. Only his legs are still inside.

A steward, Nigel Ogden, grabs his legs. And holds on. When his arms give out, two more crew take over.

They think the captain is dead. They hold on anyway, so the body won't hit the engine.

The co-pilot flies alone, and lands at Southampton. Twenty-two minutes later.

And the captain? Alive. A broken arm, a broken wrist, frostbite. He went back to flying.

So why did the window leave? The day before, a manager had replaced it. Ninety bolts. Eighty-four of them were
too thin, by less than a millimetre. Six were too short.

How? He matched the new bolts to the old ones. The old ones were wrong too.

On this plane the windscreen is fitted from the outside. So the air in the cabin pushes it outward, against
the bolts. Thinner bolts, less grip. At seventeen thousand feet, the cabin pushed harder than they could hold.

Nobody had checked his work.

## Sources
- Fabian, Sondhi, Allen, Theobald & Lin 2024, "Why flying insects gather at artificial light", Nature Communications 15: 689 (published 30 Jan 2024; Imperial College London, Florida International University, Monteverde). Field work: high-speed infrared cameras filmed wild insects around UV lights (hanging and standing, pointing in different directions) at field sites in the Monteverde Cloud Forest, Costa Rica (incl. the CIEE Monteverde campus); motion capture in the lab. Insects did not head straight for the light: they turned their back (dorsum) toward it, which produced orbiting, stalling and inverting; over an upward-pointing light they flipped over and dived to the ground. Proposed mechanism: the dorsal light response (keep your back to the brightest area, normally the sky) is hijacked by a nearby lamp. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10827719 ; https://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/251217/flying-insects-become-disorientated-trapped-artificial/ ; https://news.fiu.edu/2024/millennia-old-mystery-about-insects-and-light-at-night-gets-a-new-explanation ; https://www.ciee.org/go-abroad/college-study-abroad/blog/ciee-monteverde-campus-used-insect-and-light-research-study
- A white sheet hung above a light (glowing like a sky) let insects fly through without being trapped: Science News coverage of the study. https://www.sciencenews.org/article/insects-artificial-light-moth-direction-pollution
- The Moon idea (insects steer by the Moon and mistake lamps for it) was the most common explanation; heat attraction is also widely discounted. https://www.cnn.com/2024/02/07/world/moths-drawn-to-light-behavior-study-scn
- Simplified: "released slow-motion films in 2024" = the paper and its videos were published in 2024 (filmed earlier); "the brightest part of the night was the sky, never the ground" = the sky is normally brighter than the ground; "thousands of years" = the "millennia-old mystery" of the FIU / Florida Museum release. The compass-and-magnet analogy is ours.

## Voice-over (227 words)
1. In 2024, scientists released slow-motion films of moths around a lamp at night, in a Costa Rican cloud forest.
2. People have watched moths fly into flames and lights for thousands of years, and never agreed why.
3. The usual answer: they're drawn to the light, or they think it's the Moon.
4. But on camera, they didn't fly at the lamp. They flew around it, again and again.
5. Clue one. As they circled, they kept their backs turned toward the light.
6. Clue two. Over a lamp pointing up, they flipped upside down and fell to the ground.
7. Clue three. When a white sheet glowed above them, like the sky, they flew straight through.
8. So why would an insect turn its back to a light?
9. For millions of years, the brightest part of the night was the sky, never the ground.
10. So insects keep their backs toward the brightest light. That's how they know which way is up.
11. Near a lamp, "up" points sideways. The insect tilts toward it, turns, and ends up circling the lamp.
12. Fly over the lamp, and "up" points down. So it rolls over, and falls.
13. It's like a compass next to a magnet: nothing inside is broken. It's just pointing the wrong way.
14. Moths aren't attracted to the light. They're trapped by it.
15. And in 2024, those films from the cloud forest showed exactly that.

In 2024, scientists released slow-motion films of moths around a lamp at night, in a Costa Rican cloud forest.
People have watched moths fly into flames and lights for thousands of years, and never agreed why.
The usual answer: they're drawn to the light, or they think it's the Moon.
But on camera, they didn't fly at the lamp. They flew around it, again and again.
Clue one. As they circled, they kept their backs turned toward the light.
Clue two. Over a lamp pointing up, they flipped upside down and fell to the ground.
Clue three. When a white sheet glowed above them, like the sky, they flew straight through.
So why would an insect turn its back to a light?
For millions of years, the brightest part of the night was the sky, never the ground.
So insects keep their backs toward the brightest light. That's how they know which way is up.
Near a lamp, "up" points sideways. The insect tilts toward it, turns, and ends up circling the lamp.
Fly over the lamp, and "up" points down. So it rolls over, and falls.
It's like a compass next to a magnet: nothing inside is broken. It's just pointing the wrong way.
Moths aren't attracted to the light. They're trapped by it.
And in 2024, those films from the cloud forest showed exactly that.

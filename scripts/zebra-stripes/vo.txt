In 2016, scientists in England dressed horses in zebra-striped coats. Then they watched the flies.
Because nobody could agree why zebras have stripes at all.
The obvious answer: camouflage, to hide from lions.
But on open grassland, lions spot zebras easily. Past fifty metres, they can't even see the stripes.
Maybe stripes keep them cool? But barrels wrapped in zebra skin got just as hot.
Clue one. Zebras have the boldest stripes where biting flies are worst.
Clue two. Horse flies spread deadly diseases, and zebra hair is too short to stop a bite.
Clue three. On the horses in costume, fewer flies landed, except on their bare heads.
So what do stripes do to a fly?
A horse fly comes in fast, then slows down at the last moment to land.
On a plain horse, it brakes and touches down.
On stripes, it doesn't slow down. It bumps into the coat or zooms past.
The stripes scramble its sense of where the surface is.
Farmers in Japan painted stripes on cows. Far fewer bites.
Like stairs painted with stripes: you can't tell where to put your foot.
The stripes aren't hiding the zebra. They're hiding the landing spot.
And on those striped horses in 2016, the flies kept missing.

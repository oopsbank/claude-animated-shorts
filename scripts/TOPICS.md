## 1. Why the Toilet Hates Your Shower
- **Hook:** "Someone flushes a toilet downstairs, and the person upstairs suddenly gets blasted with hot water." (15 words) ⚠ How hot depends on the water-heater setting; show a yelp and steam, not an injury, and never say "scalded".
- **Mechanism:** The toilet tank refills from the same cold-water pipe as the shower. For a few seconds it steals cold water, the shower's mix loses cold but keeps all of its hot, and the temperature spikes. Analogy: two kids sharing one milkshake with two straws. When one starts slurping, the other suddenly gets nothing. Payoff: modern "pressure-balancing" valves now cut the hot side to match.
- **Facts:**
  - A refilling toilet draws from the shared cold supply; the shower's cold pressure drops and a basic valve lets the hot side keep flowing, so it runs hot.
  - A pressure-balanced valve uses a diaphragm or piston to reduce the other side to match. ⚠ It can take a second or two to react; don't claim it fixes the problem instantly. ⚠ Avoid naming any code or country rule.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure-balanced_valve
  - https://www.mentalfloss.com/live-smarter/home/why-flushing-toilet-changes-shower-temperature
  - https://www.rotorooter.com/blog/toilet/why-a-shower-goes-hot-when-a-toilet-is-flushed
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (bathroom + a NEW cutaway-house cam showing two floors and pipes); props: toilet, shower, towel, milkshake + two straws; FX `splash`, steam puffs (new `steam`), `sweat_drop`, `exclaim`, a water-flow arrow in the pipe (new).
- **Visual: 8/10.** A cutaway house with blue and red pipes makes cause and effect one continuous camera move, from the flush down the pipe up to the shower. The scream gag is easy slapstick.

## 2. Why the Freezer Door Won't Reopen
- **Hook:** "A freezer door you just closed is harder to open than one shut all day." (15 words)
- **Mechanism:** Opening the door lets warm room air in. Once it's sealed, that air cools fast, its pressure falls below the room's, and the outside air presses the door shut until a little air leaks back in through the seal. Analogy: a bottle of hot water you cap and put in the fridge; later the plastic is crumpled inward.
- **Facts:**
  - Manufacturers (LG, Electrolux, Haier) describe it as normal: warm air that gets in cools and lowers the pressure; it evens out after about 30 s to a few minutes; some freezers have a pressure-relief vent.
  - Gas physics: in a fixed volume, pressure falls in proportion to absolute temperature (Gay-Lussac's law).
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.lg.com/ae/support/product-help/CT20076062-1431689537126
  - https://support.electrolux.co.uk/support-articles/article/my-electrolux-fridge-freezers-door-is-difficult-to-open
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay-Lussac%27s_law
  - http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Kinetic/idegas.html
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (kitchen); props: freezer, ice-cream tub, a crushed plastic bottle; FX air "molecule" dots (new `air_dots`), `sweat_drop`, `anger_vein`, `dust_puff` (frost puff).
- **Beat plan (escalation):** one hand → two hands → a foot on the wall → (exaggeration joke, clearly cartoonish) a crowbar → two people tugging → the mystery beat → air-dots mechanism → payoff: everyone gives up, and 30 s later a toddler opens it with one finger (the door never opens on its own).
- **Visual: 7/10.** The escalating tug-of-war is clear slapstick and the shrinking-air dots show the mechanism; the beat plan fills 50 s.

## 3. Why Surgeons Once Soaked Your Hands
- **Hook:** "A finger with a cut nerve stays perfectly smooth after an hour in the bath." (14 words) The story is the nerve test, not "why fingers prune".
- **Mechanism:** Bath wrinkles are not skin soaking up water. Nerves tell the blood vessels under the fingertip to tighten, the pad loses volume, and the skin on top buckles. If the nerve is cut, the message never arrives and the finger stays smooth. Analogy: a fitted sheet on a mattress that suddenly shrinks. The sheet didn't grow; what's under it got smaller.
- **Facts:**
  - Lewis & Pickering (1935) noted that fingers with damaged nerves don't wrinkle; O'Riain (1973) formalised the "wrinkle test" (warm-water immersion, roughly 5-30 min).
  - Phelps & Walker, *Am J Occup Ther* 31(9):565-72 (1977): in recent complete nerve lacerations, absence of wrinkling matched loss of other sensation; in compression injuries it did not. ⚠ So the test is reliable only for a clean cut. Say "a cut nerve", never "any nerve damage".
  - Wilder-Smith & Chow (2003): water-immersion wrinkling is due to vasoconstriction driven by sympathetic nerves.
  - ⚠ *Why* we wrinkle is debated: Kareklas et al. 2013 (*Biol. Lett.*) found that wrinkled fingers moved wet marbles faster, but Haseleu et al. 2014 (*PLoS ONE*) found no grip benefit. Only hint at it ("maybe for grip").
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrinkle_test
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/907018/
  - https://www.nature.com/articles/nature.2013.12175
  - https://www.ncl.ac.uk/press/articles/archive/2013/01/getagrip-whywegetwrinklyfingersandtoes.html
- **Needs:** sets NEW `clinic` (a 1930s hospital ward) or `interior` (bathroom); props: basin, towel, a cutaway fingertip (new), marbles; FX `splash`, a nerve "message" pulse running down the arm (new `signal_pulse`), `sparkle`, `question_mark`.
- **Visual: 8/10.** The cutaway fingertip shrinking under the skin is very clear, and "nerve cut = no message" is one literal shot. It needs one custom cutaway graphic.

## 4. Why a Dripping Tap Goes "Plink"
- **Hook:** "The drop hitting the water makes no sound; the plink comes a moment later." (14 words)
- **Mechanism:** When a drop hits the water it punches a little crater; the crater snaps shut so fast that it traps a tiny air bubble underneath. That bubble wobbles, and its wobble shakes the water surface like a loudspeaker, which makes the "plink". Analogy: blowing across a bottle, where it's the trapped air that sings, not your breath. Payoff: a squirt of dish soap changes the surface and silences it.
- **Facts:**
  - Phillips, Agarwal & Jordan, "The Sound Produced by a Dripping Tap is Driven by Resonant Oscillations of an Entrapped Air Bubble", *Scientific Reports* 8, 9515 (2018), Cambridge: the impact, crater and jet were effectively silent; the trapped bubble's oscillation drives the water surface, which radiates the sound; the frequencies match the bubble's natural frequency.
  - Changing surface tension (adding dish soap) can stop the sound, per the authors.
  - ⚠ Keep the soap payoff as "can" and frame it as a fun tip, not a guaranteed fix for every sink.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-27913-0
  - https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/what-causes-the-sound-of-a-dripping-tap-and-how-do-you-stop-it
  - https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/06/180622104736.htm
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (kitchen or bathroom at `night`) + a NEW macro "under the water" plate (blue, simple); props: tap, sink, pillow over the head, dish-soap bottle, a bottle for the analogy; FX `splash`, sound-wave rings (new), a zoom bubble (new `zoom_bubble`), `anger_vein`, `music_note`.
- **Visual: 8/10.** A sleepless night gag (pillow, earplugs, bucket) gives escalation, and the slow-motion underwater bubble shows the mechanism literally.

## 5. Why Your Bike Steers Backwards First
- **Hook:** "Every time you turn your bike left, you first steer right without knowing it." (14 words) Told for an everyday cyclist, second person; motorbikes appear only in the payoff (safety schools teach it on purpose).
- **Mechanism:** A tiny steer to the right pulls the tyres out from under the bike, so the bike leans left, and a leaning bike turns toward its lean. Cyclists and riders do it without noticing, and motorcycle safety schools teach it on purpose ("push left, go left"). Countersteering applies to bicycles too (Wikipedia). Analogy: balancing a broom on your hand. To move the top left, you first flick your hand to the right.
- **Facts:**
  - Countersteering is a brief steer in the direction opposite the turn that starts the lean; the bike then turns toward the lean.
  - The Motorcycle Safety Foundation and racing schools teach it.
  - ⚠ The full physics (trail, gyroscopic effects, camber thrust) is argued over in detail. Keep the voice-over to "the tyres move out from under the bike, so it leans". ⚠ At walking speed the effect is masked, so keep the story at road speed.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countersteering
  - https://ridermagazine.com/stayin-safe-countersteering/
  - https://motorcyclistonline.com/clearing-up-confusion-over-how-a-motorcycle-turns
- **Needs:** sets `road` (`roadside` + a NEW top-down cam); props: bicycle sprite (side and top views), motorbike sprite for the payoff, helmet, broom; FX `speed_lines`, a steering-arrow overlay (new), `dust_puff`, `sweat_drop`.
- **Visual: 8/10.** Top-down tyre tracks show the small wiggle the wrong way; the broom analogy is one shot. The motorbike sprite is new work.

## 6. Why Rivers Eat Only One Bank
- **Hook:** "Year after year, a river bend eats a farmer's field and builds new land across the water." (16 words) The story is the river and the farmer; the teacup is the "You see," analogy.
- **Mechanism:** Water swinging round a bend piles up against the outer bank; near the riverbed friction slows it, so a slow current runs along the bottom from the outer bank to the inner one, scouring mud from the outside and dropping it on the inside. Analogy: stir loose-leaf tea, and the leaves gather in the middle of the cup, carried by the same bottom current. Payoff: in 1926 Einstein used exactly this teacup to explain rivers.
- **Facts:**
  - The tea-leaf paradox: a primary swirl plus a friction-driven secondary flow along the bottom carries leaves to the centre (Wikipedia; flow-visualisation reports).
  - Einstein, "Die Ursache der Mäanderbildung der Flussläufe und des sogenannten Baerschen Gesetzes", *Die Naturwissenschaften* 14, 223 (1926), presented 7 Jan 1926: the same secondary flow erodes the outer bank of a river bend and deposits on the inner bank.
  - ⚠ Secondary flow was described before Einstein (Isaachsen 1896, Boussinesq), so never say Einstein "discovered" it; say he "used the teacup to explain rivers". ⚠ Keep Baer's law and the Earth's rotation out; they are disputed.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_leaf_paradox
  - https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0512114 (review: Einstein's contributions to earth sciences)
  - https://bibnum.education.fr/sites/default/files/analyse-einstein-tea.pdf (analysis of the 1926 paper)
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (`table_close`) + `village` (the river, from a high cam, `golden`); props: teacup, spoon, loose tea, a big chalkboard; FX swirl-arrow overlay (new `flow_arrows`), `sparkle`, `question_mark`, `dust_puff` (mud on the river bank).
- **Visual: 8/10.** A cutaway cup with flow arrows is one clean picture, and the jump from cup to river (the same arrows on a river bend) is a strong camera transition.

## 7. Why Drier Sandcastles Stand Taller
- **Hook:** "Pour more water on a sandcastle and it collapses; add just a splash and it towers." (16 words)
- **Mechanism:** A tiny bit of water forms little bridges between grains. Its surface tension pulls the grains together like glue (capillary bridges). Too much water drowns the bridges and the sand flows. Analogy: hair: dry it's fluffy, damp it clumps into spikes, but under the shower it goes flat and limp. Payoff: the best mix is just "a few per cent" water, enough *in theory* for a castle about 5 m tall on a wide, packed base.
- **Facts:**
  - Pakpour, Habibi, Møller & Bonn, "How to construct the perfect sandcastle", *Scientific Reports* 2, 549 (2012): optimal strength at a liquid volume fraction of about 1% (the press release says "a few per cent"; use that wording); the maximum column height grows with the 2/3 power of the base radius.
  - The University of Amsterdam press release: at the optimum, "sandcastles reaching five metres in height" can be built. ⚠ Coverage mixes "1% by volume" and "by weight"; say "about one spoonful per bucket" only after checking the arithmetic, or simply "a tiny bit of water".
  - Payoff option: with water-repellent sand, the same team could build a sandcastle *underwater*, with air as the glue.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.nature.com/articles/srep00549
  - https://www.uva.nl/en/content/news/news/2012/08/how-do-you-make-the-perfect-sandcastle.html
  - https://www.nbcnews.com/science/cosmic-log/recipe-16-foot-tall-sandcastles-revealed-flna920242
- **Needs:** sets `harbour` (beach strip, `day` and `golden`); props: buckets, spade, watering can, sandcastles (three versions: dry heap, mud puddle, tall tower); FX `dust_puff`, `splash`, `sparkle`, a magnified grain bubble with water bridges (new `zoom_bubble`).
- **Visual: 8/10.** Three castles collapsing in different ways are pure slapstick, and the zoom bubble on two grains glued by a water bridge shows the mechanism literally.

## 8. Why You Sleep Badly in Hotels
- ⚠ **Below the bar:** review r4 scored originality 5-6 (widely covered on short-form video). Pick only if you like it anyway; otherwise use a reserve.
- **Hook:** "On your first night in a hotel, you sleep worse, even in the best bed." (15 words) ⚠ The study was in a sleep lab, i.e. "a new place": say "anywhere new", not "hotels" specifically, in the mechanism.
- **Mechanism:** On the first night somewhere new, the left half of the brain sleeps more lightly in deep sleep, reacts more to strange sounds, and wakes you faster. By the second night it relaxes. Analogy: campers taking turns on night watch. One stays half-awake so the others can sleep.
- **Facts:**
  - Tamaki, Bang, Watanabe & Sasaki, *Current Biology* 26 (2016) 1190-1194 (Brown University): the first-night effect shows asymmetry between the hemispheres in slow-wave sleep; the left default-mode network was more vigilant, responded more to unusual sounds, and woke people faster. The effect was gone on night two.
  - ⚠ The "protective night watch" purpose is the authors' hypothesis: say "scientists think". Don't state the participant count (sources differ across experiments).
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(16)30202-4
  - https://www.weforum.org/stories/2016/04/trouble-sleeping-in-a-new-place-heres-why-say-scientists/
  - https://www.discovermagazine.com/trouble-sleeping-in-new-places-its-probably-your-brains-fault-42187
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (a hotel bedroom at night) + `village` `night` (a campfire for the night-watch analogy, cut away to it for action); props: bed, suitcase, alarm clock, ice machine; FX a half-lit brain (new `half_glow`), sound "beep" icons, `exclaim`, `sweat_drop`.

## 9. Why Ice Cubes Have Cloudy Hearts
- ⚠ **Below the bar:** review r4 scored originality 5-6 (widely covered on short-form video). Pick only if you like it anyway; otherwise use a reserve.
- **Hook:** "Freeze the cleanest water you can find, and the ice cube still comes out cloudy." (15 words)
- **Mechanism:** Water holds dissolved air, but ice holds almost none. A cube in a tray freezes from every side inward, so the growing ice keeps shoving the air into the shrinking liquid centre, until the centre freezes full of tiny bubbles that scatter light. Analogy: sweeping a room from all four walls at once: all the dust ends up in one pile in the middle. Payoff: bartenders freeze ice from the top down only (in an insulated cooler), so the air is pushed to the bottom and they simply saw the cloudy part off.
- **Facts:**
  - Ice can hold far less dissolved air than liquid water; freezing from the outside in pushes it to the last liquid, the centre, where it forms bubbles that scatter light.
  - Minerals are a minor cause; boiling removes some air but does not make clear ice on its own.
  - Directional freezing (insulated sides, open top) moves the freezing front one way, so the air ends up at the bottom and is cut away.
  - ⚠ Don't claim boiling "does nothing"; sources disagree on how much it helps. Keep it out.
- **Sources:**
  - https://engineering.purdue.edu/MSE/AboutUs/GotMaterials/Other/yoder.html
  - https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/2090-what-is-clear-ice-and-how-can-i-make-it-at-home
  - https://indianapublicmedia.org/amomentofscience/the-secret-behind-getting-clear-ice-cubes.php
  - https://khymos.org/2008/04/13/ice-cubes-and-air-bubbles/
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (kitchen, `table_close`) + a bar counter variant (`golden`/`night`); props: ice tray, freezer, water jug, insulated cooler, saw, cocktail glass; FX a cutaway cube with an inward freezing front and herded bubbles (new `freeze_front`), `sparkle` (clear ice), `question_mark`, `dust_puff` (the sweeping analogy).
- **Visual: 8/10.** A cutaway cube where the ice walls close in and herd bubbles to the middle is a literal timelapse; the bar payoff (saw off the cloudy slab) is a strong visual twist.

## 10. Why Cereal Clumps Together in Milk
- ⚠ **Below the bar:** review r4 scored originality 5-6 (widely covered on short-form video). Pick only if you like it anyway; otherwise use a reserve.
- **Hook:** "The last few cereal rings in your bowl always drift together, then cling to the edge." (16 words)
- **Mechanism:** Each floating ring presses a small dent into the milk's surface, held up by surface tension. A neighbouring ring sits on the slope of that dent, so it slides "downhill" into it, and the two dents merge into a bigger one; near the wall, the milk curves up, so rings float up the slope to the edge. Analogy: two people lying on a soft mattress keep rolling into each other's dip.
- **Facts:**
  - Vella & Mahadevan, "The Cheerios effect", *Am. J. Phys.* 73 (9), 817-825 (2005): surface tension bends the liquid around each floating object, and that deformation plus each object's net buoyancy/weight makes them attract; cereal near the wall rides up the meniscus.
  - ⚠ Say "cereal rings", never the brand name, on screen or in voice. ⚠ No exact force numbers.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheerios_effect
  - https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0411688 (the paper's preprint)
  - https://www.livescience.com/9350-cereal-science-floating-objects-stick.html
  - https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna9425907
- **Needs:** sets `interior` (`table_close`, a breakfast table at `day`); props: bowl, milk, cereal rings, spoon, a mattress for the analogy; FX a side cutaway of the milk surface with dents (new `zoom_bubble`), `sparkle`, `question_mark`, `sweat_drop` (chasing the last rings with a spoon).
- **Visual: 8/10.** A high cam on the bowl shows rings gliding together; the side cutaway of dents and the mattress analogy are both literal.

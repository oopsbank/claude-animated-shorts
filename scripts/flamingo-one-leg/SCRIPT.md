## Sources
- Chang & Ting 2017, "Mechanical evidence that flamingos can support their body on one leg with little active muscular force", Biology Letters 13(5): 20160948 (Young-Hui Chang, Georgia Tech; Lena Ting, Emory; both Atlanta). Flamingo cadavers, held at the lower leg, passively supported their body weight on one leg in a stable posture with no muscle activity; a stable joint configuration was only found with the foot directly under the body (one-legged stance), and the cadaver could not hold a stable two-legged pose. Live juvenile flamingos at Zoo Atlanta on a force plate swayed least when quiescent (eyes closed / dozing) vs alert. Chang calls it a "passively engaged gravitational stay": body weight pushes the hip and knee against their range-of-motion limits. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233 ; https://theconversation.com/scientists-balanced-a-dead-flamingo-on-one-leg-to-unlock-the-birds-standing-secret-78231 ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/neuromechanics-of-flamingos-amazing-feats-of-balance/
- Anderson & Williams 2010, Zoo Biology 29: 365-374: flamingos rest on one leg more often in water, supporting heat saving (thermoregulation). This is WHY they may do it; the script keeps it ("That may explain why") and moves on to HOW. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19637281/
- Anatomy: the backward-bending joint halfway down a bird's leg is the ankle (intertarsal joint); the knee sits close to the body under the feathers.
- Simplified: "with no muscles working" = no muscle activity in a cadaver; "the muscles barely work" = "little active muscular force" (paper title). The analogy (locked knees) is ours.

## Voice-over (224 words)
1. In 2017, scientists in Atlanta balanced a dead flamingo on one leg. It held the pose, with no muscles working.
2. Live flamingos stand like this for hours. Even asleep.
3. The usual answer: tucking one leg up keeps it warm.
4. That may explain why. It doesn't explain how. Stand on one leg with your eyes closed, and you wobble in seconds.
5. Clue one. That backward bend in the middle of the leg isn't a knee. It's the ankle. The knee is hidden up in the feathers.
6. Clue two. On one leg, the flamingo moves its foot right under the middle of its body.
7. Clue three. Live flamingos swayed least when their eyes were closed.
8. So how does a sleeping bird stay up on one thin leg?
9. With the foot under its body, all the weight presses straight down the leg.
10. That pushes the hip and knee to the end of their bend, so the leg can't fold.
11. Gravity holds it in place. The muscles barely work.
12. That's why the dead bird stayed up. On two legs, it couldn't stand steady.
13. It's like locking your knees in a long queue: your bones carry the weight, not your muscles.
14. For us, one leg is the hard way to stand. For a flamingo, it's the easy way.
15. And in 2017, a flamingo with no working muscles proved it.

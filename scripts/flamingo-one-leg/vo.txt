In 2017, scientists in Atlanta balanced a dead flamingo on one leg. It held the pose, with no muscles working.
Live flamingos stand like this for hours. Even asleep.
The usual answer: tucking one leg up keeps it warm.
That may explain why. It doesn't explain how. Stand on one leg with your eyes closed, and you wobble in seconds.
Clue one. That backward bend in the middle of the leg isn't a knee. It's the ankle. The knee is hidden up in the feathers.
Clue two. On one leg, the flamingo moves its foot right under the middle of its body.
Clue three. Live flamingos swayed least when their eyes were closed.
So how does a sleeping bird stay up on one thin leg?
With the foot under its body, all the weight presses straight down the leg.
That pushes the hip and knee to the end of their bend, so the leg can't fold.
Gravity holds it in place. The muscles barely work.
That's why the dead bird stayed up. On two legs, it couldn't stand steady.
It's like locking your knees in a long queue: your bones carry the weight, not your muscles.
For us, one leg is the hard way to stand. For a flamingo, it's the easy way.
And in 2017, a flamingo with no working muscles proved it.

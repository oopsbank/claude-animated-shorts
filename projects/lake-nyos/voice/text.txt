One night in 1986, a lake in Cameroon killed seventeen hundred people. It didn't flood. It didn't boil. It just breathed out. The next morning, the villages were silent. No fire. No damage. Just cows lying in the fields like they'd fallen asleep. Here's the crazy part. Deep under the lake, volcanic gas had been seeping in for centuries. The bottom water held it like a shaken soda, and the cold water on top was the cap. That night, something, maybe a landslide, nobody knows for sure, popped the cap. The lake fizzed. A cloud shot up, and because this gas is heavier than air, it rolled downhill, into the valleys, into the villages. People felt warm, smelled rotten eggs, and passed out. Some woke up a day later. Many never did. And the lake? Its blue water had turned deep red: iron from the bottom, rusting in the air. Today, pipes pull water up from the bottom, so the lake burps a little every day instead of all at once, like it did one night in 1986.

Ride into a left turn, and your bike steers right first, without you knowing it. Meet Dale. Twenty years on motorcycles, and he swears he steers by leaning. So a riding school hands him a strange bike. It has two handlebars: a normal one, and one bolted to the frame that can't steer. "Hold the fixed bars," says the instructor, "and take that corner." Dale laughs, rolls onto the track, and leans hard left. Almost nothing happens. He leans harder, sticks his knee out, and wiggles his hips like he's at a wedding. The bike barely turns and runs wide across the whole track. Twenty years of riding, and he can't make the corner. Wait, if leaning your body doesn't turn the bike, what does? You see, bikes turn by leaning the whole bike, and to lean it left, you steer right for a split second. That nudge pulls the wheels out from under you, so the bike tips left, then turns left. It's like balancing a broom on your palm: to tip it left, flick your hand right first. Riders do it without noticing, but the fixed bars stopped Dale's hands from doing it. Motorcycle schools teach it on purpose: push the left grip forward to go left. And yes, bicycles too. So Dale's been steering the wrong way for twenty years, and it's the only way he ever turned.

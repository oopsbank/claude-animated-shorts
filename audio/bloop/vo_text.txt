In 1997, the Pacific Ocean made a sound heard by microphones nearly five thousand kilometres apart. You see, it rose in pitch like an animal call, but no known animal is that loud. Scientists nicknamed it the Bloop. And the internet had an answer: "Something huge is down there." But no creature fits. Even a blue whale, the loudest animal alive, isn't that loud. Clue one. It came from far south, near the edge of Antarctica. Clue two. It didn't repeat like a song. It rumbled for about a minute, then stopped. Clue three. In 2005, scientists put microphones near Antarctica, and heard it again. Thousands of times a year. So what down there roars thousands of times a year, and never needs to breathe? Not a monster. Ice. Around Antarctica, giant shelves of ice crack and break away into the sea. When a slab the size of a city splits, the crack sends a shudder through the water that travels thousands of kilometres. Like an ice cube cracking in your drink. Now make that ice cube the size of a mountain. Years later, scientists made it official: the Bloop was an icequake. The monster from the deep was the sound of Antarctica breaking. And every day, the ocean still carries that sound to anyone listening.

In 1908, a blast a thousand times stronger than Hiroshima hit Siberia. It left no crater. You see, it flattened eighty million trees across a forest bigger than London. Sixty kilometres away, a man was thrown off his porch, his shirt burning hot. Nineteen years later, scientist Leonid Kulik went hunting for the meteorite. He was sure: find the crater, find the iron. Clue one. There was no crater. No hole. No rock. Clue two. The fallen trees all pointed outward, away from one spot. Clue three. Right at the centre, the trees were still standing. Stripped bare, like telegraph poles. So what explodes, flattens a forest, and leaves the middle standing? People guessed a comet, antimatter, even a crashed alien ship. The answer: a space rock the size of a building burst apart about eight kilometres up. It never hit the ground. Only its blast did. Under the blast, the shockwave came straight down, so trees just lost their branches. Further out, it hit them sideways and knocked them flat. Like a hairdryer pointed down at grass: right underneath it stays up, everything around gets blown outward. Kulik never found his iron. The rock never touched the ground. It left no crater. Just a ring of fallen trees around bare ones, pointing at the sky.
